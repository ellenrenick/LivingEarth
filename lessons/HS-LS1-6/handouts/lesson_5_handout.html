Lesson 5: Small Pieces, Big Molecules

Focus question: How do amino acids become proteins, and what happens to them when an animal eats?

Warm-up

A pumpkin makes thousands of different proteins, but it only uses about 20 kinds of amino acids. How is that possible?



Part 1: Monomers and polymers

A monomer is a small molecule that can link with others. A polymer is a large molecule made of many linked monomers.

Activity B: Eat, digest, and rebuild

You eat a handful of roasted pumpkin seeds. Your body needs to build new hair protein.

  1. Digest: Unhook every clip. Your digestive system breaks proteins into single amino acids. Your blood carries them to your cells.
  2. Rebuild: Using only the clips from the pumpkin protein, try to build this short, simplified piece of a human hair protein:
    K – Q – Q – L – V – Q – T – T – L – Q – V – K
Amino acidVLTKQ
How many does the hair protein need?
Short or extra? (+ or −)

If you're short, you may take clips from the “cafeteria” cup (you ate other food today too). Put extra clips in your recycle tray.

  1. Is your hair protein the same as the pumpkin protein? Explain.
  2. Where did most of the atoms in your hair protein come from? Go back as far as you can.
  3. What happens to the extra amino acids your body doesn't need? Are those atoms destroyed?
