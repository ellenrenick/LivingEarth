Station 1: Following labeled carbon

What they did: In the 1950s, scientist Melvin Calvin and his team gave tiny green algae carbon dioxide made with a special “labeled” carbon atom that can be detected. The algae were in bright light. After different amounts of time, the scientists stopped the algae and found which molecules contained the labeled carbon. Calvin won the Nobel Prize for this work.

Time after adding labeled CO2Where most of the labeled carbon was found
5 secondsA small molecule with 3 carbon atoms, the first molecule the algae build from CO2
30 secondsThe small 3-carbon molecule and sugars
5 minutesSugars, plus amino acids and other small carbon-based molecules
Later experiments with plants (hours to days)Starch, proteins, fats, and cell walls

Data simplified from Calvin's experiments and later studies.

Expert questions

  1. Where did the labeled carbon show up first?
  2. List the order in which the labeled carbon appeared in different molecules.
  3. What does the order tell you about where the carbon in amino acids and proteins comes from?
  4. The scientists could follow the same carbon atoms from molecule to molecule. What does that tell you about atoms?

Station 2: Tomatoes without nitrogen

What they did: Students grew tomato seedlings in water with dissolved nutrients (no soil). Both groups got the same light, water, CO2, and temperature. One group got all nutrients. The other got all nutrients except nitrogen. After 4 weeks, they measured the plants.

Expert questions

  1. Could the plants without nitrogen still do photosynthesis? What evidence tells you?
  2. Which measurements were much lower without nitrogen? Give numbers.
  3. Explain why sugar built up in the plants without nitrogen. Use what you know about the atoms in sugar and in amino acids.
  4. The plants without nitrogen had pale leaves. Think back to the chlorophyll card from Lesson 2. Why might that be?

Station 3: Squash, fungi, and phosphorus

What they did: The roots of most plants, including squash and pumpkins, partner with fungi. The fungi grow long, thin threads that spread far into the soil. Scientists grew squash in soil with very little phosphorus. Some plants had the fungi on their roots and some didn't. Everything else was the same.

Experiment A: after 6 weeks

MeasurementWithout fungiWith fungi
Phosphorus in leaves (mg/g)1.23.0
Sugar in leaves (mg/g)2214
Dry mass (g)4.09.0

Experiment B: Scientists gave the leaves of squash plants with fungi carbon dioxide made with labeled carbon. Two days later, they found the labeled carbon in the fungus threads out in the soil, built into the fungus's own fats and other molecules.

Did you know? Plants need phosphorus to build DNA, RNA, cell membranes, and ATP (a molecule that carries energy in cells).

Expert questions

  1. What does the fungus get from the plant? What does the plant get from the fungus? Use both experiments.
  2. Give two pieces of data showing that the fungi helped the plants.
  3. Name two molecules a plant can't build without phosphorus.
  4. The plants with fungi had less sugar in their leaves. Give two possible reasons.
  5. Where did the carbon in the fungus's molecules originally come from? Trace it back as far as you can.

Station 4: Crops with extra CO2

What they did: In outdoor experiments called FACE (Free-Air CO2 Enrichment), scientists ran pipes around real crop fields and released extra CO2 into the air. Some fields also got plenty of nitrogen fertilizer, and some got very little. They measured how much the crops grew.

Growth (compared to 100 = normal CO2, plenty of nitrogen)Normal CO2 (about 400 ppm)Extra CO2 (about 600 ppm)
Plenty of nitrogen100118

Expert questions

  1. How much did extra CO2 increase growth with plenty of nitrogen? With low nitrogen?
  2. Explain how extra CO2 in the air could make a plant grow more. What molecule does the plant build from it?
  3. Why was the boost from extra CO2 so much smaller when nitrogen was low?
  4. What does this experiment suggest about where much of a plant's mass comes from?
